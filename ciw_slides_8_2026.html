8.1 The End of Global Poverty?

The Goal(s)

1.1 By 2030, eradicate extreme poverty for all people everywhere[…]

United Nations, 2015. Agenda for sustainable development.

percentage of people living [in extreme poverty] to fall to no more than 3 percent globally by 2030

World Bank, 2014. End Extreme Poverty and Promote Shared Prosperity.

Historic Progress in Poverty Reduction

  • partly driven by ‘moral imperative’, these goals were known to be ambitious
  • Worldwide extreme poverty fell dramatically over the last generation
  • decline concentrated in places that achieved sustained growth, especially in Asia
  • poverty reduction slowed sharply around COVID-19; showing uneven recovery
  • extreme poverty is now concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) and fragile or conflict-affected settings
  • Yet: “End poverty by 2030” is now far off track under current projections

Measuring Global Poverty

Global poverty estimates involve:

  • survey data on household income or consumption; consumption often includes valued home production
  • welfare measure: household income or consumption per person (equilvance scales)
  • poverty line: a common purchasing-power-adjusted benchmark, comparable across countries
  • poverty index: headcount, depth, severity (FGT), or some other statistic

current WB international poverty lines

  • extreme poverty: $3.00 p.p.p.d. 2021 PPP-adjusted dollars
    • previous: $2.15 p.p.p.d. (2017 PPP)
    • slight but visible upward jump in poverty so defined (statistical artifact)
  • $4.20 and $8.30 for lower-middle- and upper-middle-income-country standards

The Decline of (Extreme) Poverty Across Time and Space

  • Facts
  • FGT0 and FGT1
  • Space
  • Persistence
  • after 1990: large secular progress of extreme poverty reduction made in absolute terms
  • 1990: \(\approx\) 2.3b people below current international poverty line (43% of World pop.)
  • 2025: \(\approx\) 0.8b (\(\approx\) twice EU population; 10% of World pop.)
  • East and South Asia experienced large poverty reduction
  • Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) now accounts for largest share of extreme poverty
    • often fragile and conflict-affected countries
  • rapid population growth: absolute number of people in poverty can rise even though rate falls
Headcount Ratio
Poverty Gap Ratio
  • GDP growth is part of the answer to poverty reduction, but growth is unequal across countries, may not benefit everyone, and may not last
  • important constraints keep countries and people in pockets of poverty
    • Low and volatile productivity growth: especially in agriculture and informal work
    • Conflict and fragility: displacement, destroyed assets, disrupted services, and weak state capacity
    • Demography and geograhpy: fast population growth, remoteness, and limited connectivity
    • Low human capital and thin markets: health, education, credit, jobs, and public services interact
    • Repeated shocks: food and energy price crises, climate hazards, diseases, and macro shocks

8.2 Setbacks and Reorientation

COVID19 Pandemic

  • Facts
  • Picture
  • The COVID19 Pandemic was met with unprecendented speed of vaccine development, but distribution was delivered very unevenly
  • The pandemic produced the first substantial global rise in extreme poverty in a generation
  • World Bank estimates indicate roughly 50m more people in extreme poverty between 2019 and 2020
  • recovery to pre-pandemic trajectory was incomplete, especially low-income countries were affected
    • but tricky: what would have happened to poverty trajectory without the Pandemic?
  • post-pandemic inflation and geopolitical shocks compounded losses, particularly for households relying on food and fuel

What Happened to the 2030 Ambition?

  • the original ambition was to end extreme poverty by 2030
  • this goal is far off track under current growth projections
  • hundreds of millions are projected to remain in extreme poverty
  • with weak growth in the poorest economies, the remaining count may fall very slowly—or even rise
  • formally, the UN has not given up on SDG.1
  • however, re-orientation in narrative:

“renew and strengthen its commitment to poverty eradication in the decades ahead. Poverty must remain at the centre of global development efforts beyond 2030.”

World Bank Scenario Updates

Shared Prosperity

  • Measure
  • Interpretation

  • The World Bank’s country-level shared prosperity measure tracks growth in average income or consumption of the bottom 40 percent of a country’s distribution

  • related UN-SDG 10.1 ambition: income growth for the bottom 40 percent should be sustained at a rate higher than the national average

  • Share of income of bottom 40% (‘B40’) \[\bar{y}^{B40} = \dfrac{1}{N_{B40}} \sum_{i=1}^{N_{B40}} y_i \]

Strengths

  • makes incidence of growth visible, not just national mean
  • provides clear country-level distributional objective

Limitations

  • country-specific; it is not directly aggregable into one global bottom-40 growth rate
  • an average for the bottom 40 gives relatively more influence to people near the 40th percentile than to the very poorest
  • needs comparable household-survey data, which are often infrequent
  • says little by itself about poverty depth, vulnerability, or access to public services

The Global Prosperity Gap

  • GPG
  • Example
  • Distribution
  • GPG asks how far income/consumption distribution is from a prosperity benchmark \(z\)

\[ GPG_z = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{z}{y_i} \]

  • shows: average factor by which each person’s resources would have to be scaled to reach the benchmark. Unlike a poverty headcount, it uses information from the full distribution.
  • \(z\) is currently at US$28 per person per day
  • GPG is decomposable as the product of $28/mean income and inequality: index decreases (or shared prosperity improves) as mean income increases, or as inequality decreases

Source: Kraay, Aart at al. (2023). A New Distribution Sensitive Index for Measuring Welfare, Poverty, and Inequality. Policy Research Working Paper. World Bank

  • Suppose daily incomes are US$2.50, US$5, US$12.50, US$25, and US$50

  • prosperity benchmark is \(z=\)US$25.

  • The individual scale-up factors are 10, 5, 2, 1, and 0.5.

\[ GPG = \frac{10+5+2+1+0.5}{5}=3.7 \]

  • headcount ratio below US$25 is 3/5 = 0.6
  • but it treats the first three people equally
  • poverty gap ratio below US$25 is 0.44
  • it is focused on the poor, and measures the distance from the threshold
  • GPG distinguishes how far each is (multiplicative factor) from the benchmark and also includes the two people above it

Conclusions

Summary

  • UN and WB goals of ending or eradicating extreme poverty (based on a $3 p.p.p.d PPP-adjusted benchmark) were very ambitious at the time of writing (ca. 2014)

  • world has indeed witnessed historical decline of extreme poverty

  • the 2020-22 COVID pandemic flipped the downward trend at least temporarily and may have structurally retarded the speed

  • 2030 is fully out of reach, not only for eradication but also for the 3% WB goal

  • shared prosperity index, focusing on the bottom 40% of countries, or a global prosperity index, move away from extreme poverty

  • progress is measurably there, but has slowed, and may flip

  • there are enough many countries, especially in SSA region, and troubled by armed conflict and political instability for whom ending poverty and entering an age of prosperity is remote

  • task ahead is not only to reduce poverty, but to make poverty escape durable

  • ending poverty remains ambitious goal, requiring sustained growth, inclusive distribution, and strong political institutions to build resilient liberal democracies

  • countries also need fiscal capacity to finance services and offer social protection

  • climate risk major threat to some of the poorest countries on earth, requiring international cooperation and support
