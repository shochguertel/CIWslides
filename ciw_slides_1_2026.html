Economics Joins Models and Data

Different tools answer different parts of a policy or research question.

Economic models

  • Clarify mechanisms, incentives, constraints, and trade-offs.
  • Make assumptions explicit.
  • Derive counterfactual implications.

Statistical data

  • Describe populations and outcomes.
  • Reveal heterogeneity, trends, and anomalies.
  • Check whether model-relevant patterns are present.

Policy judgment

  • Combines evidence with objectives.
  • Requires welfare weights, feasibility, and institutional knowledge.
  • Is not supplied automatically by a statistic.

How might a carbon tax change production and consumption?

What is the average price elasticity of electricity demand?

Which distributional effects of a gas price shock are acceptable?

Inferential Statistics

  • Inference
  • Hypotheses
  • Errors
  • statistical inference is that area of statistics that asks:
    • what can be learned about a (large) population if only have a finite (small) random sample of size (\(n\))?
  • formulate hypotheses about some true population parameter
  • measure or estimate the magnitude of the corresponding sample moment
  • use statistical tests to infer the validity of the hypotheses
  • use a limit distribution of the test and some \(p-\)value to make statements about statistical significance
  • judge whether to reject or accept the hypothesis
  • statistical hypotheses dissect the parameter space into a Null and its Alternative: \(H_0\) and \(H_A\) (or \(H_1\))
    • e.g. for parameter \(\theta\): \(H_0: \theta=0 \qquad H_A: \theta\neq 0\)
  • a statistical test makes statements as to whether there is enough evidence to refute \(H_0\) in favor of \(H_A\)

Never Accept

You never formally accept a null; at most, you fail to reject.

  • formulating hypotheses can be more complex, for instance,
    • if there is a joint hypotheses about many parameters
    • if alternatives are one-sided

a decision on \(H_0\) with the given sample can lead to two types of error

decide\(\to\) reject \(H_0\) not reject \(H_0\)
\(H_0\) is true type I error correct decision
probability: \(\alpha\) \(1-\alpha\)
\(H_0\) is false correct decision type II error
probability: \(1-\beta\) \(\beta\)
  • type I error (false positive): reject \(H_0\) even though true
  • type II error (false negative): not reject \(H_0\) even though false
  • \(\alpha\) is chosen by the researcher
  • often, \(\alpha=0.05\) (or, \(5\%\))
  • \(\alpha\) then signifies the size of the test: the max. probability of falsely rejecting a true \(H_0\)
  • \(\beta\) is a feature of the test, often, one wants \(\beta\le 0.2\) (or, \(20\%\))
  • \(1-\beta\) signifies the power of the test: probability of correctly rejecting a false \(H_0\)

trade-off

  • lowering \(\alpha\) increases \(\beta\) (i.e. chance of not rejecting a false \(H_0\))
  • increasing sample size \(N\) increases power and lowers both \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)

Aspects of Inference

A sample can describe an estimated population quantity—if the sampling and measurement support it.

Possible

Estimate a population mean, share, median, or difference between group means.

Example: Estimate the share of households below a relative poverty threshold, with a confidence interval.

Conditions

  • Defined target population
  • Probability sampling or credible approximation
  • Appropriate survey weights
  • Attention to nonresponse and measurement error
  • Uncertainty reported, not hidden

Not implied

A sample difference does not establish why groups differ.

“Benefit recipients have lower employment” may reflect eligibility, selection, local labor markets, health, or incentives.

Statistical inference from sample to population is different from causal inference from observed differences to policy effects.

Testing: Means

  • Sample
  • Hypotheses
  • Simple \(t\)-test
  • \(t\) vs \(z\)
  • consider sample data of size \(n\)
  • values \(x_i\) of a variable \(x\) will vary in the sample, \(i=1,\ldots,n\)
  • sample distribution can be characterized by sample statistics such as
    • mean: \(\qquad \qquad \qquad m = \dfrac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^n x_j\)
    • standard deviation \(\quad s = \left(\dfrac{1}{n-1} \sum_{j=1}^n (x_j-m)^2\right)^\frac{1}{2}\)
  • perhaps, we expect a certain value for the mean, how can we test?
  • two important parameters to choose before testing:
    • confidence, or significance level \(\alpha\)
      • implies wrongly rejecting a true \(H_0\) occurs with probability \(\alpha\) or less
    • one- or two-sided tests
      • e.g. \(H_A: \mu\neq\mu_0\;\) (two-sided) \(\quad\) or \(\quad\) \(H_A: \mu>\mu_0\;\) (one-sided)

assume

  • random sample of size \(n\) from
  • an underlying, normally distributed population

hypothesize: \(\qquad \qquad H_0: \mu=\mu_0\quad \qquad\) versus \(\qquad \qquad H_A: \mu\neq\mu_0\)

also assume: known standard deviation \(\sigma\)

test statistic \[z = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \sim {\cal N}(0,1)\]

\(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\) is known as standard error (s.e.)

\({\cal N}(0,1)\) is the standard normal distribution

instead of known \(\sigma\), use estimate \(s\)

test statistic \[t = \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \sim {\cal t}(n-1)\]

\(n-1\) is degrees of freedom (d.o.f.)

\(t()\) is the Student \(t\)-distribution

  • \(t\)-distribution converges to a normal when d.o.f.\(\to\infty\)
  • for \(n\) large enough, at 5% (10%) significance level, one rejects hypothesis \(H_0\) that \(\mu\) is equal to \(\mu_0\) if test statistic falls in the (colored) rejection area of \(\pm 1.96 (1.645)\) / outside conf.interval

Summary and Conclusions

Descriptive statistics are necessary, but usually insufficient, for policy evaluation.

Important

Descriptives help to:

  • Formulate the question
  • Diagnose data quality
  • Reduce data in meaningful way
  • Assess external relevance
  • Communicate heterogeneity

importance: often first step in credible empirical analysis

Not Prediction

A descriptive association does not tell us how well we can forecast new observations.

Prediction needs:

  • A defined target
  • Out-of-sample evaluation
  • A loss criterion

A trend line is not automatically a forecasting model.

Not Causal

Correlation, group differences, and before–after changes can be driven by:

  • Confounding
  • Selection
  • Reverse causality
  • Common shocks

Causality needs randomized experiments and/or construction of counterfactuals.
